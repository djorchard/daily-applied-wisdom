Team Topologies

Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais

2019Software engineering · Organisational design · Leadership5–8 minute read

Treat team boundaries and interaction patterns as part of the software-delivery system, constrained by finite cognitive capacity.

Evidence lens. Team Topologies is a practitioner framework, not a scientifically proven recipe for high performance. Limited working memory is well supported, but applying cognitive-load theory to whole teams is an organisational extension rather than a directly established causal law. This lesson uses the 2019 first-edition vocabulary; the 2025 second edition clarifies that a platform is often a grouping of team types delivering a compelling internal product, not necessarily one isolated team.

01

Idea 1

Treat cognitive load as an architectural constraint #

The idea

The authors argue that a team should own a coherent amount of software, domain knowledge, operational responsibility, tooling and business context that its members can realistically understand.

When responsibility grows too broad, delivery slows because more effort goes into navigating complexity, switching context and reconstructing knowledge. Team boundaries should therefore reflect cognitive limits, not just reporting lines.

Applied interpretation. This resembles designing software for finite memory. Declaring ownership does not increase processing capacity; each application, integration, stakeholder group and specialist technology consumes part of a finite attention budget.

Why it matters

A workload problem can masquerade as a performance problem. Pressure may worsen context switching, forgotten details and fragile ownership when the real issue is excessive cognitive breadth.

Apply it today

List everything one team must understand to make a safe production change: rules, repositories, databases, deployment, infrastructure, integrations, support, approvals and stakeholders.

Circle the item consuming substantial mental effort but contributing least to the core value stream. Consider simplification, automation, platform support or reassignment.

Caveat or failure mode

Cognitive load can become a vague excuse for avoiding difficult work. Some complexity is intrinsic to the domain and should remain close to the people making the relevant decisions.

02

Idea 2

Make stream-aligned teams the centre #

The idea

The first-edition framework defines four fundamental team types. Stream-aligned teams own a meaningful flow of work and deliver value end to end. Enabling teams build capability, complicated-subsystem teams contain specialist complexity, and a platform capability offers internal services that reduce unnecessary complexity. Later guidance emphasises that the platform may be a grouping of several team types operating as an internal product.

The supporting types exist to improve the stream-aligned team's flow, not to recreate permanent functional silos with new labels.

Applied interpretation. Ask what arrangement lets a team move from a user need to a working outcome with the fewest blocking dependencies, not which department traditionally owns a technology.

Why it matters

Functional silos can optimise specialist utilisation while creating queues between specialists. A stream-aligned design instead makes the flow of change the primary unit of optimisation.

Apply it today

Take one recent enhancement and map its path from request through analysis, development, infrastructure, approval, deployment and support.

Mark where work stopped for another team. Target the handoff with the most waiting relative to actual work, not simply the busiest-looking group.

Caveat or failure mode

End-to-end ownership does not require every specialist inside every team. Deeply specialised components can justify a complicated-subsystem team, and a good platform can avoid duplicating expertise.

03

Idea 3

Give every relationship an explicit interaction mode #

The idea

Team Topologies names three deliberate interaction modes: collaboration for high-bandwidth discovery, X-as-a-Service for consuming a capability through a clear interface, and facilitation for temporary capability-building help.

Collaboration is intentionally expensive and should usually be time-bounded. A common path is to collaborate while uncertainty is high, then evolve toward a clearer service relationship when boundaries stabilise.

Applied interpretation. Many dependencies hurt because nobody has decided what the relationship is supposed to become. Naming the current mode and its exit condition turns 'work closely together' into a design decision.

Why it matters

Without an exit condition, teams can spend months in each other's meetings and coordinate every change. Explicit modes make the dependency inspectable and adaptable.

Apply it today

Complete: For the next month, our relationship with Team X should be ___ because ___.

Then complete: We know the interaction can change when ___. If the last blank is impossible, the dependency may have no exit condition.

Caveat or failure mode

Real relationships can combine modes and change by task. Use the vocabulary to clarify intent, not to force every interaction into a permanent classification.

Question 1 of 6 · Idea 1 · ConceptWhy should cognitive load influence team and architecture boundaries?

The framework does not provide a standard ownership limit; manageable boundaries depend on the complexity and context involved.

Each domain, system and dependency consumes attention, so responsibility can exceed a team's capacity for safe, coherent change.

Cognitive load helps inspect complexity, but it does not by itself decide which work should move; some difficult domain work belongs with the team.

Responsibility creates knowledge and coordination costs even before it creates tickets, so ticket volume alone is too narrow.

A team needs enough working knowledge to change its area safely. Cognitive load is a diagnostic constraint, not an exact capacity formula or an excuse to avoid essential complexity.

Question 2 of 6 · Idea 2 · ConceptWhat chiefly distinguishes a stream-aligned team from a traditional frontend, database or other functional team?

The team boundary follows a flow of value rather than a single technical discipline, reducing blocking handoffs.

A product grouping can still depend on another team to coordinate its value flow; stream alignment puts that flow at the centre of ownership.

A value stream can serve internal users or operational outcomes, and stream-aligned teams may own more than interface work.

Centralising a discipline describes a functional silo, which can improve specialist utilisation while creating queues between teams.

Stream alignment makes value flow the primary boundary. Supporting team types and platform capabilities reduce dependencies; end-to-end ownership does not require duplicating every specialist.

Question 3 of 6 · Idea 3 · ConceptWhat is the practical difference between collaboration and X-as-a-Service?

Documentation may support a service, but the distinction is interaction shape and coordination cost rather than formality.

Collaboration supports joint learning under uncertainty; X-as-a-Service reduces routine coordination through a defined capability and interface.

Ownership alone does not set the mode; teams can collaborate across an ownership boundary until a consumable interface exists.

Temporary capability transfer describes facilitation; X-as-a-Service instead provides a capability without joint implementation of routine work.

Collaboration buys learning but is coordination-heavy. When boundaries stabilise, a service relationship can reduce that cost; facilitation is the mode aimed at temporary capability transfer.

Question 4 of 6 · Idea 1 · ApplicationA capable team owns nine applications with different deployments, databases and stakeholders. What should a leader investigate before asking it to work faster?

Capacity balancing can expose workload, but ticket hours omit the knowledge and coordination burden created by broad ownership.

Prioritisation may help temporarily, but unchanged deployments, databases and stakeholders preserve much of the cognitive breadth.

Measuring task switching is useful, but personal targets do not remove system complexity and may encourage local optimisation.

Mapping the team's knowledge burden reveals where simplification, platform support or boundary changes could reduce unnecessary load.

The symptoms may come from excessive cognitive breadth rather than effort. Reducing unnecessary complexity or reshaping ownership addresses the mechanism causing slow, fragile delivery.

Question 5 of 6 · Idea 2 · ApplicationA feature takes two days to build and then waits six days for infrastructure and deployment groups. What should be examined first?

A one-day build saving has limited end-to-end effect while the six-day dependency remains the dominant delay.

Steadier forecasts may help planning, but maximising specialist utilisation can preserve or enlarge the handoff queue.

The largest waiting period points to a boundary or service problem that self-service or a clearer interaction could reduce.

Release-by-release embedding may reduce some waiting, but it preserves coordination and does not create a scalable service relationship.

The largest delay lies in the dependency, so inspect the boundary and service around it. Platform support can reduce waiting without requiring every specialist inside every stream-aligned team.

Question 6 of 6 · Idea 3 · ApplicationAfter six months of joint discovery, the deployment steps are now stable and repetitive, but two teams still execute each deployment together because one owns the capability. What is the strongest next design move?

Time-boxing is better than permanent collaboration, but the stable work now provides evidence to design the lower-coordination relationship directly.

Teaching direct tool operation may transfer skill, but it can also expose platform complexity instead of providing a clear service boundary.

Meetings remain high-coordination joint work; documenting them does not create a consumable deployment capability.

A defined interface or self-service path turns what the teams learned together into a lower-coordination service relationship.

The current work is high-bandwidth collaboration, regardless of tool ownership. Codifying the capability and its interface creates a credible path to lower-coordination service consumption.

Today's experiment · 7 minutes

Run a dependency audit

  1. Open the work item or release consuming the most coordination effort.
  2. List every external team required before value reaches the user.
  3. Mark each C for collaboration, S for X-as-a-Service, F for facilitation or ? for unclear.
  4. Count the question marks and choose the highest-friction unclear relationship.

Observable result: You finish with a number and one proposed interaction mode with an exit condition.

One-sentence takeaway

A team's performance depends on how much complexity it owns, where its boundaries sit and whether its dependencies have a deliberate shape.
