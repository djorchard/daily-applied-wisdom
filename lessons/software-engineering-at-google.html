Software Engineering at Google

Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck and Hyrum Wright

2020Software engineering · Engineering leadership5–8 minute read

How to keep software changeable, understandable and economically sustainable as years, teams and dependencies accumulate.

Evidence lens. This is a practitioner account of Google's engineering practices, not a universal recipe. Its strongest value is as a set of explicit trade-offs tested at unusual scale; smaller organisations should adapt the mechanisms to their own lifespan and coordination costs.

Idea 1

Engineer for change, not just correctness today #

The idea

The authors distinguish programming from software engineering by adding time, scale and trade-offs. Programming asks whether code works now. Software engineering also asks whether the organisation can still change that code safely and economically throughout its useful life.

Their definition of sustainability is practical: a project is sustainable when it retains the capability to respond to valuable technical or business changes. It does not require performing every possible upgrade.

Applied interpretation. Maintainability is preserved optionality. A system is healthier when future engineers can alter it without heroic knowledge, risky rewrites or frozen dependencies.

Why it matters

A shortcut can be rational for a three-week prototype and reckless for a ten-year business system. Expected lifespan should influence investment in tests, interfaces, automation, documentation, migration paths and consistency.

Apply it today

Pick one component you expect to survive for several more years. Name one plausible change that would be disproportionately difficult: a vendor replacement, identity change, data migration, new client or new regulatory rule.

Write the smallest move that would preserve the option to make that change later. Do not build the future feature; reduce one source of avoidable lock-in.

Caveat or failure mode

Future-proofing can become speculative over-engineering. Preserve capability for plausible changes with asymmetric consequences, not every imaginable future.

02

Idea 2

Observable behaviour becomes part of your API #

The idea

Hyrum's Law states that, with enough users of an API, every observable behaviour will eventually be depended on by somebody, whether or not it was documented as part of the contract. Ordering, error text, timing, default values, whitespace, side effects and quirks can all become dependencies.

The practical lesson is not that APIs can never change. It is that the real compatibility surface is usually larger than the promised one.

Applied interpretation. Treat compatibility as an observation problem before treating it as a documentation argument. Telemetry, consumer tests and staged migrations reveal dependencies that the written contract cannot.

Why it matters

Apparently harmless refactors can break production consumers because compatibility risk grows from what users can observe, not merely what the specification promises.

Apply it today

Choose one API, database view, file export or shared component. List three observable behaviours that are not explicitly guaranteed.

Classify each as safe to change, change only with telemetry or migration, or effectively part of the contract now.

Caveat or failure mode

Hyrum's Law is an engineering observation, not a mathematical law. A small system with known consumers can often change behaviour safely after checking them directly.

03

Idea 3

Code review is a knowledge-distribution system #

The idea

The book treats code review as more than defect detection. A well-designed process also improves comprehension, consistency, shared ownership, knowledge transfer and the historical record of why a change was made.

Google's account explicitly acknowledges the cost: review adds latency and can become unsustainable when it is heavyweight. Small changes, prompt feedback and automation keep the human attention focused on judgement.

Applied interpretation. Review reduces key-person risk only when it transfers context. The asset being distributed is not approval; it is enough understanding for someone else to maintain and challenge the change.

Why it matters

When only one developer understands an area, every future change queues behind that person. Review can gradually spread comprehension and make ownership less fragile.

Apply it today

For the next meaningful review, ask: What assumption does this change rely on that the next maintainer is least likely to know?

If it matters, move that rationale into the code, test, change description or durable documentation. A review-only explanation may disappear from where future readers need it.

Caveat or failure mode

Mandatory review can become ceremony or a gatekeeper queue. Optimise for small changes, fast first responses, clear reviewer roles and automated checks; do not confuse more reviewers with better review.

Question 1 of 6 · Idea 1 · ConceptWhat most clearly distinguishes software engineering from programming in this lesson?

Reusable abstractions can help, but using more of them does not by itself address change across time, scale and organisational cost.

Present correctness and delivery cost matter, but software engineering also considers whether valuable future change remains economical.

Time, scale and trade-offs extend the concern from working code today to sustainable change throughout the software's useful life.

Building anticipated upgrades commits to predictions; sustainability instead preserves options for plausible changes without implementing them early.

Software engineering stretches a coding decision across time and organisational scale. Sustainability means retaining the capability to respond to valuable change, not predicting or implementing every future.

Question 2 of 6 · Idea 2 · ConceptAccording to Hyrum's Law, which behaviour can become part of an API's practical compatibility surface?

The contract defines promised behaviour, but consumers can still form dependencies on additional details they observe.

Provider tests describe intended behaviour but do not reveal assumptions made independently by consumers.

Internal details affect maintenance, but they do not become consumer compatibility dependencies unless they are observable through the API.

Observable details can acquire consumers, so practical compatibility often extends beyond the written contract.

Dependencies form around observations, not documentation boundaries. Ordering, timing, defaults or error text can therefore acquire compatibility costs once consumers rely on them.

Question 3 of 6 · Idea 3 · ConceptHow can code review reduce key-person risk beyond catching defects?

Review can build understanding gradually, but approving one change does not transfer the full context needed to own a component.

Sharing context and rationale gives more people the understanding needed to maintain and challenge the code.

Centralising decisions with one senior engineer preserves key-person dependence and can create a delivery bottleneck.

Review history is useful evidence, but it cannot substitute for rationale placed where future maintainers will need it.

Key-person risk falls when another maintainer gains enough context to understand and challenge the change. Approval alone does not transfer that knowledge.

Question 4 of 6 · Idea 1 · ApplicationAn internal service is expected to run for eight years, but vendor API calls are scattered throughout its code. Replacement is plausible but not scheduled. What is the best next move?

Several adapters still pay now for uncertain providers. A single boundary preserves the replacement option with less speculative cost.

A tested boundary reduces avoidable lock-in while postponing provider-specific investment until evidence justifies it.

Documentation helps discovery, but leaving calls distributed preserves the structural migration cost that the boundary is meant to reduce.

A licence comparison omits internal maintenance, migration and opportunity cost, so it does not establish that owning the capability is the better trade-off.

A narrow seam preserves the valuable option to replace the vendor without paying for a speculative platform today. It addresses plausible lock-in while limiting over-engineering.

Question 5 of 6 · Idea 2 · ApplicationYou want to change an internal endpoint's result ordering because no order was promised. What should you do before shipping?

Consumer evidence distinguishes a safe change from one requiring staging, communication or migration.

Notice helps manage a change, but it does not reveal which consumers depend on ordering or whether they can migrate safely.

Hyrum's Law warns about hidden dependencies, but it does not require preserving observed behaviour when evidence supports a managed change.

Changing the written contract does not remove dependencies on current behaviour; consumer evidence is still needed before deployment.

The real question is whether consumers rely on the order. Evidence from usage and tests supports either a safe direct change or a managed migration; Hyrum's Law does not require freezing every behaviour.

Question 6 of 6 · Idea 3 · ApplicationA senior developer reviews nearly every change because they know the system best, but work now queues behind them. Which response best preserves review while spreading knowledge?

A delayed summary may spread awareness, but it removes timely challenge and context transfer from the change itself.

Rotating observation exposes more people, but an additional approval gate increases latency without giving them active review responsibility.

Smaller changes and automation lower review effort, while coaching and durable rationale broaden future ownership.

Scheduled batches reduce interruption frequency but increase review complexity, feedback delay and the cost of understanding each change.

Small reviews and automation reduce review cost, while guided participation transfers system context to more people. Larger batches or extra gatekeepers intensify the bottleneck.

Today's experiment · 10 minutes

Run a changeability scan

  1. Choose one long-lived component you actively own.
  2. Write one plausible change it may face in the next two years.
  3. Identify the single assumption or dependency that would make that change hardest.
  4. Write one follow-up action that would take less than a day to implement later.

Observable result: Finish this sentence: If X changes, Y is our biggest obstacle, so the next cheap move is Z.
