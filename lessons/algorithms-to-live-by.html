Algorithms to Live By

Brian Christian and Tom Griffiths

2016 · first editionApplied mathematics and quantitative reasoning · Algorithms · Decisions under uncertainty5–8 minute read

Use mathematical problem structure to decide when to stop searching, how much uncertainty to explore and which scheduling rule fits the cost you actually want to reduce.

Evidence lens. Christian and Griffiths translate established results from computer science, operations research and decision theory into everyday heuristics. The mathematics is strong inside each model's assumptions; the everyday application is a practitioner interpretation and becomes context-dependent when options are not random, values change, choices can be revisited or human costs resist a single objective. Use the models to expose structure and trade-offs, not as precision theatre.

Idea 1

Separate learning from choosing #

The idea

The book uses the secretary problem to teach optimal stopping. Under strict conditions, a known number of options arrives in random order, each rejected option is gone, only relative rank matters and the goal is to maximise the chance of choosing the single best. The optimal rule samples roughly the first 37 per cent without selecting, then chooses the next option better than every sampled option.

The durable idea is not that every search ends at 37 per cent. It is that search has two jobs: learn the distribution and act before opportunity disappears. Naming the assumptions prevents a clean result from being copied into decisions with recall, changing quality, unknown horizons or several acceptable outcomes.

Applied interpretation. Before a bounded search, reserve an explicit calibration phase and define a threshold for the choice phase. Write which real conditions violate the classical model and adjust the rule rather than hiding the mismatch.

Why it matters

Without a planned learning phase, the first plausible option can anchor the search. Without a stopping rule, every new option preserves doubt and delays value. A threshold makes the trade-off explicit.

Apply it today

Choose one upcoming search with a real deadline and estimate how many comparable options you can inspect.

Reserve an early sample for calibration, define what later option would beat its benchmark and list any reason rejected options could be revisited.

Caveat or failure mode

The 37 per cent rule is optimal only for a narrow objective and harsh no-recall assumptions. Hiring, housing and relationships involve ethics, thresholds, unequal information, negotiation and repeat opportunities. Do not turn a mathematical thought experiment into an automatic human policy.

Idea 2

Let the horizon price exploration #

The idea

The explore-exploit trade-off appears in multi-armed bandit problems. Exploitation chooses the option with the best observed reward; exploration tries an uncertain option that might teach enough to improve later choices. The value of information depends on how many decisions remain.

With a long horizon, learning about an uncertain alternative can repay its short-term cost many times. Near the end, there are fewer future choices through which to recover that cost, so using the best-supported option becomes more attractive. Uncertainty is therefore not automatically a reason to avoid an option; it can be the source of information value.

Applied interpretation. For a repeated decision, record the estimated value, uncertainty and remaining opportunities. Explore when the decision recurs enough for learning to matter; exploit when the horizon is short or failure cost exceeds the information gained.

Why it matters

Teams often argue about innovation as a personality preference. Horizon and information value turn part of that argument into a decision model: how many future choices can use what this experiment teaches?

Apply it today

List one repeated choice, the current favourite and one uncertain alternative whose result would change later decisions.

Estimate how many uses remain and choose a small exploration only if the learning can be reused enough to justify its risk and cost.

Caveat or failure mode

Bandit models simplify changing environments, dependent choices, delayed feedback and unequal harms. Exploration is not permission to impose risk on others without consent or safeguards, and a long horizon does not make every experiment worthwhile.

Idea 3

Choose the scheduling rule for the cost #

The idea

Scheduling theory shows that there is no universally best order without an objective. If the aim is to minimise average completion time for available jobs, shortest processing time first performs well. If the aim is to minimise maximum lateness against due dates, earliest due date first fits that objective. A priority rule is an optimisation claim, whether or not it is named as one.

The book also describes thrashing: when switching and coordination consume so much capacity that little useful work completes. In overloaded systems, restricting work in progress or batching related tasks can improve throughput even when every waiting item appears urgent.

Applied interpretation. Before ordering work, name the cost: lateness, average waiting, missed value, risk or context switching. Apply one rule for that cost, protect completion and review whether the metric creates harms the model omitted.

Why it matters

Arguments about priority often hide different definitions of success. Making the objective explicit reveals why two sensible people choose different orders and whether constant switching is preventing either rule from working.

Apply it today

Take four current tasks and write duration, due date and cost of delay for each.

Order them once for average completion and once for lateness, then choose the objective that matters now and limit simultaneous starts.

Caveat or failure mode

Durations and costs are estimates, urgent safety work may override a queue and human tasks are not interchangeable jobs. Optimising one metric can starve large work, neglect fairness or reward gaming, so use ageing, review points and explicit exceptions.

6 questions not yet answered.

Question 1 of 6 · Idea 1 · ConceptWhat is the most transferable lesson of the book's optimal-stopping example?

The exact proportion belongs to the classical model. Real searches need an assumption check before any numerical rule is borrowed.

Searching for proof that nothing better exists can consume the horizon. Optimal stopping accepts that waiting also has a cost.

Early commitment may be rational in some contexts, but it sacrifices information without first establishing a benchmark.

The structure separates learning from choosing and keeps the elegant number conditional on its assumptions.

The secretary result makes the learn-versus-act trade-off concrete. Its useful transfer is a deliberate calibration phase and stopping threshold, accompanied by an honest check of horizon, recall and objective assumptions.

Question 2 of 6 · Idea 2 · ConceptWhy does a longer horizon usually make exploration more valuable?

The possible learning can be reused across more future decisions, increasing its expected value.

Known performance may drift, but the general horizon effect comes from how many later choices can benefit from new information.

Exploration can reveal that the uncertain option is worse. Its value is expected information, not guaranteed reward.

A longer horizon spreads potential benefit across more choices, but the exploratory attempt still consumes time and can fail.

Exploration pays by producing information, and that information has more opportunities to affect later choices when the horizon is long. It remains costly and uncertain, so the model does not guarantee a better immediate or eventual reward.

Question 3 of 6 · Idea 3 · ConceptWhy can two mathematically sensible scheduling rules produce different task orders?

Both rules can operate with estimates, and estimation error matters. Their central difference is the objective each order serves.

Different target costs yield different optimal or useful priority rules for the same queue.

The underlying scheduling ideas apply to both human and machine work, with extra human constraints requiring care.

Start and finish definitions matter operationally, but they do not explain the main difference between these priority rules.

A schedule is evaluated against an objective. Shortest processing time and earliest due date solve different cost problems, so disagreement about order may really be disagreement about what the system should minimise.

Question 4 of 6 · Idea 1 · ApplicationA team will review twelve vendor proposals in random order and cannot revisit a rejected proposal. Which plan best uses the model cautiously?

The model does not select at the end of the sample automatically. It uses the sample to set a threshold for later options.

Waiting until every proposal expires defeats the no-recall problem: the information arrives, but no opportunity remains to choose.

Calibration establishes a comparison threshold, and the choice phase acts when a later option beats it.

A perfect-score threshold may leave no selection and optimises a different objective. Minimum standards should be explicit but realistic.

The plan uses early options to calibrate and later options to act, which matches the model's structure. The numerical cut-off remains approximate, and real procurement still requires minimum standards, due diligence and ethical process.

Question 5 of 6 · Idea 2 · ApplicationA product team will make the same deployment choice fifty times this year. An untested method might cut recovery time. What is the best first move?

An early controlled trial can repay its learning across many future uses while limiting the cost of being wrong.

Exploitation protects known value, but avoiding every uncertain option gives up information that could improve a long sequence of choices.

A full switch confuses exploration with commitment. The point is to buy information with a bounded amount of risk.

Late information has almost no remaining decisions to improve, so the horizon makes the same trial less valuable.

A long repeated horizon gives an early, bounded trial high information value. Safeguards keep exploration proportionate; a full switch would expose every deployment before the team has learned whether the method works.

Question 6 of 6 · Idea 3 · ApplicationA support team starts every newly urgent ticket and now finishes almost nothing. Which intervention best addresses the scheduling failure?

More labels can describe the queue but do not restore capacity lost to too many simultaneous starts.

Restricting active work reduces switching overhead, while an explicit queue rule makes the cost trade-off visible.

Faster switching still pays setup and attention costs and can deepen the pattern of activity without completion.

Touch counts reward visible busyness and may make thrashing look successful even as customers wait longer for completed work.

The symptom is thrashing: switching and coordination are consuming capacity while completion collapses. A work-in-progress limit protects finishing, and a named queue objective handles waiting work more coherently.

Today's experiment · 10 minutes

Run one decision through three algorithms

  1. Choose a bounded search or repeated decision and write its horizon, recall rules and success objective.
  2. Mark what an early calibration sample would teach and the threshold that would trigger action.
  3. Name one uncertain option and how many later choices could use information from a small trial.
  4. List four waiting tasks, order them for average completion and for lateness, then circle the objective you actually need today.

Observable result: You finish with one explicit stopping threshold, one priced exploration and a queue order tied to a named cost.

One-sentence takeaway

Do not copy a clever number: identify the decision structure, state the cost and assumptions, then use the simplest algorithm that makes the trade-off visible.
