The Design of Everyday Things

Don Norman

1988 · revised 2013Product design · Human behaviour · Usability5–8 minute read

When users repeatedly make the same mistake, the interface may be teaching the wrong action.

Evidence lens. Norman's framework combines established ideas from cognitive psychology with practical design principles. It is strongest as a diagnostic language for discoverability, mental models and error resilience, not as proof that one interface pattern works for every user or culture.

Idea 1

Make the right action visible #

The idea

Norman distinguishes affordances, which are relationships that make actions possible, from signifiers: perceptible clues showing where and how to act. Signifiers became more prominent in the revised edition because digital interfaces can support many invisible actions.

A control can technically afford an action while remaining functionally undiscoverable. Repeated requests for help with an ordinary task may be evidence of weak signalling rather than weak users.

Applied interpretation. In software, wording, position, grouping, state and feedback all signify. A familiar icon can still fail when its placement or visual priority tells the wrong story.

Why it matters

A user should not need to reverse-engineer the designer's intention. Clear signifiers reduce hesitation, support learning and prevent repeated support questions.

Apply it today

Pick one screen and identify its most important next action. Imagine a first-time user with no documentation: what visible cue tells them what to do?

If the answer is mostly 'they should know', strengthen one cue and define an observable measure such as completion rate, hesitation time or support contacts.

Caveat or failure mode

More signalling is not automatically better. Labels, icons, badges and hints can compete until none has priority. Aim for enough information for the intended action to be inferred correctly.

02

Idea 2

Give users a correct conceptual model #

The idea

People build simplified explanations of how systems work. Good design helps them form a useful conceptual model so they can predict what actions will do and interpret system state.

Norman's thermostat example shows the cost of a wrong model: with a simple on/off thermostat, setting a much higher target does not make heating faster; it only changes when heating stops. A user can act rationally from the model the interface appears to teach and still be wrong.

Applied interpretation. Product teams often explain screens instead of repairing the system image. If users misunderstand the workflow's rules, another tooltip may treat the symptom while preserving the wrong model.

Why it matters

Once users understand how a system behaves, they can reason through unfamiliar cases. A wrong model produces systematic errors that training alone may not remove.

Apply it today

Choose one confusing workflow and write two sentences: 'Users seem to believe…' and 'The system actually…'.

If they differ, find the status name, sequence, wording or feedback teaching the wrong model.

Caveat or failure mode

Conceptual models are simplifications. Exposing internal architecture can make the product harder to understand; the model only needs enough fidelity to predict relevant behaviour.

03

Idea 3

Design for error, not perfect behaviour #

The idea

Norman rejects the habit of treating every error as careless behaviour. A slip is the wrong action while pursuing the right goal; a mistake begins with the wrong goal, rule or plan. The two need different defences.

Constraints, interlocks, undo, confirmation, sensibility checks, safe defaults and resilience are different tools. The important design question is not only how to prevent the wrong action, but how much damage occurs after it.

Applied interpretation. For consequential workflows, recoverability is a product capability. A reversible state can be more valuable than another warning because it still works when attention fails.

Why it matters

Undo, previews, reversible states and constraints can turn a serious incident into a small correction, even when a competent user acts under pressure.

Apply it today

Find one destructive, financial or hard-to-reverse action. Classify its current defence as prevented, detectable, recoverable or irreversible.

If it is mostly irreversible, identify the smallest change that makes the state recoverable.

Caveat or failure mode

Confirmation dialogs are not a universal solution. If routine actions always ask 'Are you sure?', users learn to click through. Reserve strong friction for unusual or consequential actions.

Question 1 of 6 · Idea 1 · ConceptWhat is the key difference between an affordance and a signifier?

This reverses the concepts. The affordance concerns possible action, while the signifier helps people perceive that possibility.

Affordances concern possible action; signifiers are the perceptible clues that communicate it.

Intent and outcome feedback matter, but a signifier specifically indicates where or how an action can be taken.

Affordances are relationships between an actor and an object, not merely physical counterparts to digital signifiers.

An affordance concerns what can be done; a signifier makes that possibility discoverable. A possible action can still fail users when its cues are weak.

Question 2 of 6 · Idea 2 · ConceptWhat does a useful conceptual model give a user?

Technical detail may be accurate, but a useful user model omits internal complexity that is not needed for relevant predictions.

Instructions support familiar cases, but a conceptual model also helps users reason through unfamiliar situations.

Outcome feedback reports what happened after an action; a conceptual model supports prediction before and between actions.

A useful conceptual model is simplified but accurate enough for users to predict actions, transitions and states.

A conceptual model need not mirror the implementation. It needs enough fidelity for users to predict relevant behaviour beyond memorised instructions.

Question 3 of 6 · Idea 3 · ConceptHow does Norman distinguish a slip from a mistake?

Familiarity can influence error likelihood, but the distinction rests on intention and execution rather than task novelty.

Attention and knowledge are relevant causes, but either can contribute to errors; the key distinction is where the plan goes wrong.

Slips occur during execution of an appropriate intention; mistakes begin with an unsuitable intention, rule or plan.

Feedback affects detection and recovery, but it does not determine whether the original error was a slip or a mistake.

Slips and mistakes arise through different mechanisms, so they call for different defences: execution support for slips and better models, rules or guidance for mistakes.

Question 4 of 6 · Idea 1 · ApplicationUsers repeatedly contact support because a small grey icon opens an important approval workflow. What is the best first response?

Onboarding may teach returning users, but the ordinary action remains weakly signified when and where it is needed.

A confirmation can clarify consequences after selection, but it does not help users find the control in the first place.

Clear wording, placement and emphasis strengthen the signifier, while measurement checks whether discoverability actually improves.

A tooltip adds information, but users must first discover and hover over the same weak cue; a visible label communicates the action directly.

The recurring problem is discoverability. Strengthening the signifier addresses what the interface teaches, while an observable measure tests whether the change helps.

Question 5 of 6 · Idea 2 · ApplicationUsers think Submit sends data externally, but it actually places the item in an internal validation queue. Which redesign best repairs the conceptual model?

The action label and resulting state expose the real workflow, allowing users to predict what has and has not happened.

Help can explain the distinction, but the unchanged action and state continue teaching the misleading model during everyday use.

Submission success remains ambiguous and may reinforce the belief that external sending is complete before validation finishes.

The diagram adds implementation detail, but it does not make the action's immediate result and current state as clear as direct labels do.

The action wording, visible state and feedback should teach the real sequence. Training or implementation detail leaves the misleading system image largely intact.

Question 6 of 6 · Idea 3 · ApplicationAn authorised administrator can permanently delete a customer with one click. Which redesign best limits harm without adding constant warning fatigue?

Typed confirmation adds useful friction, but an attention lapse or mistaken selection still leaves no recovery after deletion.

A recoverable state limits damage when prevention fails, while targeted confirmation preserves attention for truly irreversible removal.

Audit records and alerts support investigation, but they do not restore the customer or reduce the immediate consequence of a slip.

Training and guidance can improve understanding, but they do not reliably prevent attention failures or make errors recoverable.

Recoverability still protects the system when attention fails. Targeted friction can support consequential final removal without training users to dismiss routine warnings.

Today's experiment · 7 minutes

Run a usability archaeology test

  1. Open one screen without touching anything.
  2. Write the three actions a new user would probably think are available.
  3. Compare them with the three actions the product actually wants them to take.
  4. Circle the largest mismatch and classify it as a weak signifier, wrong conceptual model or poor error design.

Observable result: You finish with one classified design problem and one observable behaviour to measure after changing it.
