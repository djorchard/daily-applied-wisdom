Today’s book

Systems thinking · 1990

Thinking in Systems

Donella H. Meadows

Meadows gives us a compact language for seeing the structures beneath recurring events. It is especially useful when a sensible local fix keeps making the wider problem worse.

01

Idea one

Events are the surface, not the system

When a pattern keeps recurring, look beyond the latest incident for the relationships and feedback that produce it.

A system is more than its visible parts. Meadows focuses attention on stocks, flows, delays, and feedback loops: the machinery that makes behaviour persist over time. Fixing one event can be useful; changing a recurring pattern normally requires finding the structure that creates it.

Apply it today. If a delivery keeps slipping, map the loop before adding another status meeting. What work enters the queue, what leaves it, and where does rework feed back in?

Watch for. A neat diagram can still miss people, incentives, and outside constraints. Treat it as a hypothesis to test, not the system itself.

02

Idea two

Delays create surprises

A system can be responding correctly and still look broken if its feedback arrives late.

Meadows argues that delays are a major source of oscillation and over-correction. When we cannot see the effect of an action for weeks or months, we tend to push harder, then reverse too late. Shortening a delay—or making it visible—can improve a system without changing anyone’s intent.

Apply it today. Put an explicit lead-time measure on a product decision: when will the leading signal appear, and when will the customer outcome be visible?

Watch for. Faster feedback is not always better when it rewards a noisy proxy. Pair early signals with the outcome you ultimately care about.

03

Idea three

Change the rules before pushing the people

The most influential places to intervene are often the goals, information, and rules shaping everyone’s choices.

Not all interventions have equal leverage. Meadows’ influential “leverage points” framework puts parameters such as targets and budgets below deeper changes to feedback, rules, goals, and mindsets. That does not make deep change easy; it asks us to avoid mistaking activity for leverage.

Apply it today. Before asking a team to be more careful, inspect what the workflow rewards. Does the metric make speed visible but leave quality or rework invisible?

Watch for. Changing goals or rules can have broad side effects. Involve the people who live with the system and monitor the result.
